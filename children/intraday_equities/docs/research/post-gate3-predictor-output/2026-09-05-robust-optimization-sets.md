# Estimating robust-optimization uncertainty sets from the predictor

## Question

If a return/alpha predictor feeds a robust or distributionally robust portfolio/order-selection program after Gate 3, how should its uncertainty set be estimated? Which uncertainty belongs to the predictor, which must be learned empirically, and how should box, ellipsoidal, budgeted, polyhedral, scenario, moment, or Wasserstein sets be calibrated?

## Finding

The uncertainty set should describe **out-of-sample joint forecast error at the decision horizon**, conditional on information available when the trade is placed. It should not be constructed directly from the predictor’s in-sample fit, feature importance, or ordinary coefficient confidence intervals.

Let the predictor output \(\hat\mu_t\) and the realized return be \(r_t\). Build the calibration sample from strictly out-of-sample residual vectors \(e_t=r_t-\hat\mu_t\), generated by rolling-origin or purged walk-forward prediction. Include execution slippage or fill uncertainty separately if the optimization selects orders rather than only target weights. Estimate sets by horizon and, when data permit, by volatility/liquidity regime. This preserves cross-asset dependence and avoids leakage.

**What model intervals can provide.** A correctly specified predictive distribution can supply a center, conditional scale, quantiles, or covariance shape. Bayesian posterior intervals can express parameter uncertainty; bootstrap ensembles can approximate estimator instability; quantile models can estimate asymmetric tails. But these are ingredients, not sufficient validation. A confidence interval for \(E[r\mid x]\) concerns uncertainty in the conditional mean, whereas the optimizer usually faces the next realized \(r\), requiring a prediction interval that also includes irreducible noise. Marginal 95% intervals for each asset are not a 95% simultaneous region for the return vector. Their joint coverage and dependence must be estimated or calibrated empirically.

**Choice of geometry.**

- **Box:** use componentwise lower and upper residual quantiles, preferably asymmetric and volatility-scaled. It is transparent and gives LP/MILP-friendly robust counterparts, but it permits every asset to reach its adverse endpoint simultaneously. With many correlated assets this produces implausible joint scenarios, over-conservative books, and often cash or no-trade solutions. If a robust minimum-return constraint must hold over the box, it may become infeasible.
- **Ellipsoid:** estimate a robust residual center and regularized covariance or scatter matrix, then choose the radius from an empirical quantile of residual Mahalanobis distances. Ellipsoids represent dependence and typically yield second-order-cone counterparts. They are natural when errors are approximately elliptical, but covariance estimation is unstable when dimension approaches sample size and can hide skewness or multimodality. Shrinkage or factor structure is essential in high dimensions. Ben-Tal and Nemirovski establish the tractability of important robust counterparts under ellipsoidal uncertainty; Goldfarb and Iyengar connect portfolio uncertainty structures to statistical confidence regions.
- **Bertsimas–Sim budgeted uncertainty:** start with empirically estimated componentwise adverse deviations and constrain their normalized total through \(\Gamma\). This prevents all coefficients from simultaneously attaining their worst cases while retaining LP/MILP tractability. Estimate deviations from residual tail quantiles; choose \(\Gamma\) using rolling validation of violation frequency and downstream utility, turnover, concentration, and drawdown. \(\Gamma\) is a dependence/conservatism parameter, not something a predictor’s confidence interval identifies automatically. The original probabilistic bounds require distributional assumptions and should not substitute for financial backtesting.
- **General polyhedral set:** fit linear inequalities to residual scenarios, factors, clusters, or economically defensible stress directions. It can preserve asymmetry and regime structure while remaining compatible with robust linear models. Control complexity aggressively: a polytope closely wrapping a small sample overfits, whereas its full convex hull can admit unrealistic mixtures or omit unseen tails.
- **Residual scenario set:** retain blocked out-of-sample residual vectors and optimize against the finite scenarios, their convex hull, or clustered representatives. This preserves observed co-movement and tail asymmetry better than marginal intervals. It does not protect against unobserved distributions and therefore needs stress scenarios, recency weighting, regime conditioning, or an ambiguity neighborhood.
- **Mean–covariance DRO:** estimate confidence regions for the conditional mean and covariance rather than assuming they are known. Delage and Ye provide a tractable moment-ambiguity framework and data-derived confidence regions. This is appropriate when the decision depends mainly on first and second moments, but two distributions sharing those moments can have very different tail losses. Mean and covariance uncertainty should both be represented; robustifying only the mean while treating a noisy covariance estimate as truth leaves substantial estimation risk.
- **Quantile-based sets:** useful for skewed alpha errors and downside-focused objectives. Estimate joint or factor-conditional residual quantiles, not merely independent per-asset quantiles. Split-conformal calibration can convert residual scores into finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability, but financial time series violate exchangeability through dependence and drift. Coverage must therefore be checked sequentially and by regime; marginal coverage does not imply protection in the states that drive portfolio losses.
- **Wasserstein DRO:** center an ambiguity ball on the empirical distribution of out-of-sample residual vectors or joint \((x,e)\) observations. It protects against probability-mass and support misspecification and often has tractable convex reformulations. The radius is the critical estimated quantity. Select it through time-ordered validation, statistical concentration bounds, or robust Wasserstein profile inference—not by arbitrary convention. Theoretical finite-sample radii can be pessimistic in high dimensions; ordinary random cross-validation leaks temporal structure. Pflug and Wozabal introduced Wasserstein-style ambiguity in portfolio selection; Esfahani and Kuhn provide finite-sample guarantees and tractable reformulations; Blanchet, Chen, and Zhou estimate both the ambiguity radius and robust return target for mean–variance portfolios.

A practical default is: cross-fitted residual vectors; volatility normalization; a shrinkage/factor ellipsoid when a conic solver is acceptable, or budgeted polyhedral uncertainty when integer order selection requires LP/MILP structure. Keep explicit historical stress scenarios outside the fitted core set. Use Wasserstein DRO when ambiguity about the whole residual distribution matters and there is enough data to calibrate its radius.

Calibration should target the **decision**, not coverage alone. On nested rolling windows, estimate the set using training/calibration data, solve the portfolio, then measure realized set violations, constraint breaches, net utility, turnover, concentration, drawdown, and frequency of cash/no-trade or infeasibility. Select radius, confidence level, or \(\Gamma\) before the final test period. Compare against the nominal optimizer and simple shrinkage baselines.

Misspecification creates two opposite failures. Undercoverage lets the optimizer amplify false alpha through extreme positions. Overwide or poorly shaped sets erase genuine cross-sectional information, force minimum-variance/cash solutions, and make robust return floors infeasible. Diagnose unconditional and regime-conditional coverage, serial clustering of misses, residual bias, covariance drift, and sensitivity of allocations to small radius changes. If feasibility depends on uncertain return targets, use soft constraints with explicit penalties or report a robustly attainable frontier; never silently shrink the set until the requested target becomes feasible.
