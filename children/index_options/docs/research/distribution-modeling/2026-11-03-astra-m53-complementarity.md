# Why the stronger Lasso component does not produce the stronger M53

## Question and scope
Why does original full M53 beat the Lasso10 replacement on exposed VAL2020–2023? This is retrospective research over the same 449,498 eligible rows, not new validation or a promoted recipe. A0002 score, A0003 settlement boundary, A0005 uncertainty, and A0008 frozen components remain binding. No 2024+ outcomes, predictive model training, production, MIO, or repository/journal mutations. Scripts and evidence live in this WSL2 directory. The parent will journal the finding.

## Finding
Complementarity is supported, but it is not the entire explanation. A smaller component score does not imply a better incremental direction once other distributions are already present. Lasso is more redundant with existing forecasts; rolling weight adaptation adds a second observable disadvantage. Marginal calibration and shape vary by year. Quantile geometry matters in principle, but cannot alone explain a ranking that also appears under a convex CDF pool.

1. **Forecast innovation, not raw error correlation.** Use inner product E[sum_k W_k a_k b_k], with the exact 13-grid primary weights. Innovations are each CDF minus calibrated market, so common binary outcomes cancel. MLP/pooled cosine is .37376; Lasso/pooled .58201. After projecting onto pooled and baseline innovations, MLP retains 73.7942% of weighted squared energy, Lasso 58.2137%. These are squared-energy fractions, not ordinary norm percentages. MLP's remaining direction aligns with market forecast error by .00293109 (expiry/symbol clustered 95% interval [.00182345,.00403873]); Lasso .00187031 ([.00109359,.00264702]). Both retain useful information; these intervals do not test the difference between directions and ignore selection.

2. **Quantile geometry and local minima cannot be the sole causes.** Retrospective globally optimal simplex CDF pools score .526358926 without either ML component; .525295729 with MLP; .525482654 with Lasso; .525091673 with both. MLP improves the non-ML pool by .001063197, Lasso .000876271. The overall quadratic solutions are interior and independently reproduced by linear algebra, hence globally optimal for this restricted CDF class. MLP wins three annual oracle comparisons; Lasso wins 2020. Both strictly improves each annual oracle, but nesting ensures non-worsening and in-sample optimization is optimistic. This does not establish a causal CDF pool beats M53.

3. **Weight adaptation contributes independently.** Parent counterfactual replays original and rebuilt quantile CDFs within 1e-12. Original MLP/original weights score .525580619; Lasso/original weights .525759545; Lasso/refitted weights .525915636; MLP/refitted weights .525692960. Along substitution-then-refit, .0340433% loss increase comes from substitution and .0296988% from weight refit, approximately 53%/47% of the observed .0637422% gap. This attribution is path-dependent; it is not a causal estimate of a universal component/optimizer contribution. All direct paired intervals versus original include zero. Annual decomposition is heterogeneous: 2020 substitution improves loss by .0660%, then refitting worsens it by .1258%; 2021 substitution worsens by .1308%, but refitting helps by .0191%; 2022 both worsen (.0362% and .0128%); 2023 both worsen (.0297% and .00408%). Historical learning and future annual distributions differ. Weight adaptation is not uniformly harmful.

4. **Calibration is part of the story, not a complete alternative.** At original oracle weights, Lasso's pooled squared mean grid bias is smaller (.000502390 vs MLP .000555828), yet its centered error is larger (.524980265 vs .524739901). Reoptimizing after removing each threshold's observed marginal error preserves the overall MLP advantage (.524672021 vs .524926960). These centered arrays are covariance diagnostics, not valid calibrated forecast products. Annual results differ: 2021 centered pools nearly tie; 2023 favors Lasso. Do not claim uniformly better MLP conditional calibration.

5. **The lower-tail shapes differ substantially.** Average 5% quantiles are MLP -1.7325, Lasso -2.0300, pooled -2.0462, calibrated market -2.1141. MLP's realized PIT below .05 rate is 7.27%, Lasso's 4.53%. Thus MLP is not simply the better calibrated standalone forecast: its distinct, thinner left tail is useful only in a combination and may also add conditional variation. Full M53 versus Lasso-M53 average 90% width is 3.9640 versus 4.0296; lower PIT tail rates 4.55% versus 4.13%. These marginal summaries do not prove causal shape mediation or conditional calibration. Lasso's grid rearrangement is not a demonstrated defect; measuring the pre-rearranged forecast would require reconstructing it and is not warranted as the first experiment.

## Plausibility hierarchy
Highest support: useful incremental forecast directions and differing redundancy; independently corroborated by the convex pool and bias control. Also directly measured: adverse transfer of refitted weights to evaluation rows. Plausible additional contributors: lower-tail geometry and time-varying calibration, with annual reversals. Not excluded: local minima in the original nonconvex quantile objective, despite all saved optimizers converging. Low current support: data or implementation artifact, given fixed cohort, prior correctness checks, exact counterfactual replay, and diagnostic score parity. None of these findings makes the original-versus-rebuilt gap statistically significant.

## Prioritized experiments (freeze before evaluation)
1. **Retain both frozen ML components.** Compare original four-component M53 to a five-component quantile pool using identical settled-history teaching, buckets, 21-day schedule, knots and cohort. Fit only combination weights. Also include a five-component CDF-linear pool as one prespecified geometry control, not a sweep. Most direct test of the measured distinct directions. Runtime likely minutes to tens of minutes for weight-only fitting; benchmark one historical block before estimating the full run.
2. **A single weight-stability control.** Compare unregularized five-component weights to one prespecified shrinkage-to-global/history rule, with its strength chosen only from pre-evaluation settled history. Preserve score, schedule and inputs. Track weight variation and next-block scores, not just training improvement. This follows from adverse adaptation; do not assume a Bayesian label alone fixes it. Tiny convex CDF fits are cheap; quantile fits may take minutes to tens of minutes. No predictive model refit.
3. **Bounded optimization/geometry check before richer model work.** On a small deterministic set of historical learning blocks fixed in advance (e.g. first eligible block per fold and dispersion bucket), refit the existing quantile objective from equal weights, its CDF solution, and simplex vertices, retaining the best historical objective only. This separates initialization issues from model differences. Do not select blocks using the largest observed losses. Use saved forecasts; likely minutes, measured by pilot. If minima agree, stop optimizer investigation.

For a future experiment, freeze the comparison before evaluation: exact identity/cutoff parity, valid monotone CDFs, all four annual results and expiry/symbol clustered intervals. Preserve A0005's binding market and horizon-baseline comparisons; report incremental performance versus original M53 without adding a new year-specific selection gate. These are proposed experiments, not an adopted change to the owner's selection procedure. Existing exposed validation remains retrospective even with new chronological fits; no claim of fresh generalization or permission to open sealed outcomes follows. If the bounded both-component and stability controls fail, stop this branch instead of broad training/HPO. Do not reopen the previously rejected direct location/shape/gating search without a distinct approved target.

## Primary sources and their scope
- Lichtendahl, Grushka-Cockayne and Winkler (2013), [Is It Better to Average Probabilities or Quantiles?](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.1120.1667): quantile averaging and probability averaging have different dispersion geometry; the former is sharper. This motivates a controlled operator comparison, not a claim one must win here.
- Gneiting and Ranjan, [Combining Predictive Distributions](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.1638): coherence, calibration and dispersion can change under linear and nonlinear pooling; spread-adjusted and beta-transformed pools are alternatives. No evidence here yet justifies adding another calibration transform.
- Yao, Vehtari, Simpson and Gelman, [Using stacking to average Bayesian predictive distributions](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.02030): optimize the combined predictive score rather than standalone model preference; proper scoring rules and regularization fit this question. Ordinary leave-one-out is inappropriate for this time-dependent settlement setting; retain settled-history blocks.
- Yao, Pirs, Vehtari and Gelman, [Bayesian hierarchical stacking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08954): partial pooling of input-dependent combination weights is a principled candidate when model usefulness varies. Evidence here supports a small stability control, not an expensive new Bayesian base model.
- Chernozhukov, Fernandez-Val and Galichon, [Quantile and Probability Curves Without Crossing](https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.3649): rearrangement can improve monotone-curve estimates under stated metrics. It is not evidence that Lasso's sorting harms this particular combination.
